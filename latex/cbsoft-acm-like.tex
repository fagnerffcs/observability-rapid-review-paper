\begin{abstract}
The rise of cloud-native applications (CNAs) and the widespread adoption of Kubernetes (K8s) have introduced significant complexity in distributed system management. In these dynamic environments, traditional monitoring is insufficient, making observability essential for understanding internal system states through the collection and correlation of metrics, logs, traces, and platform events. This paper analyzes the state of the art in observability for Kubernetes-based environments, focusing on open-source tools and architectural patterns to minimize vendor lock-in and infrastructure costs. We employed a Rapid Review methodology adhering to established evidence-based software engineering guidelines, systematically retrieving and screening 312 candidate records from four major scientific databases published between January 2018 and October 2025. Following a rigorous multi-stage PRISMA selection protocol, 10 primary studies were synthesized. The findings reveal the scarcity of formal, unified reference architectures capable of holistically managing the observability data lifecycle. Instead, a \textit{de facto} standard has emerged around a loosely coupled open-source stack—primarily OpenTelemetry, Prometheus, Grafana, Loki, Jaeger, and eBPF-based tooling—creating notable operational complexity and correlation overhead. To address these limitations, we formalize the Observability Maturity Model (OMM) across five evolutionary levels and propose a strategic research roadmap targeting semantic interoperability, AIOps correlation, and autonomic GreenOps.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

The rise of cloud-native applications, built to leverage the distributed capabilities of cloud delivery models, is a defining feature of modern software engineering \cite{vancamp24, oracle_2021}. Solidified by architectural paradigms like the 12-Factor App\footnote{\textit{The Twelve-Factor App} is a methodology for building modern SaaS applications. Available at: \url{https://12factor.net/}.} and orchestrated as the industry standard by Kubernetes (K8s) \cite{gannon_2017, cncf_survey_2023, vancamp24}, this architectural style enables applications to operate at global scale with unprecedented elasticity. However, this architectural distribution comes at a substantial cost: structural complexity. Unlike monolithic deployments, cloud-native applications are composed of dozens or hundreds of ephemeral, containerized microservices, making fault detection and latency diagnosis orders of magnitude more challenging \cite{usman_2022}.

This inherent complexity renders traditional monitoring approaches insufficient. In dynamic Kubernetes clusters, rapid pod autoscaling, container ephemerality, and complex cross-service call graphs frequently obscure the root causes of production incidents. Consequently, \textbf{observability}—the property of estimating a system's internal states solely from knowledge of its external telemetry outputs \cite{kalman_1959, songtab_1984}—has emerged as a foundational discipline for operating cloud-native systems. Effective observability requires the coordinated acquisition, contextualization, and correlation of metrics, logs, distributed traces, and platform events.

In response to this demand, a powerful \textit{de facto} standard based on open-source tools, largely incubated and graduated by the Cloud Native Computing Foundation (CNCF), has achieved widespread industrial adoption. However, while formal and empirically validated reference architectures remain scarce in the scientific literature \cite{araujo_24, zanatta_23, kosinska_2023}, production ecosystems have coalesced around heterogeneous community tools. The central issue addressed by this paper is that this \textit{de facto} solution does not constitute a single, cohesive platform, but rather a fragmented assortment of specialized collectors, databases, and visualization interfaces.

This paper argues that this fragmentation, while offering modularity, imposes a significant operational burden, high cognitive load on engineering teams, and nontrivial resource overhead. Through a Rapid Review grounded in evidence-based guidelines, we: (1) systematically map the essential components and telemetry layers of Kubernetes observability architectures; (2) examine existing reference frameworks; (3) identify dominant open-source tools and their interoperability limitations; and (4) synthesize research gaps into an evolutionary Observability Maturity Model and research roadmap.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:background}

Cloud-native computing represents an approach to building, deploying, and operating software that fully exploits the elastic and distributed properties of cloud infrastructure \cite{aws_2025, gannon_2017}. Although cloud-native applications (CNAs) can be hosted on public clouds, private bare-metal clusters, or hybrid topologies, their runtime management typically relies on Kubernetes as the container orchestration control plane. This dynamic operational model, characterized by ephemeral pods, decoupled microservices, and automated autoscaling, leads to exponential growth in the volume, velocity, and variety of telemetry data \cite{sridharan_2018}. Managing, transmitting, and analyzing this vast telemetry stream becomes economically prohibitive and technically challenging, directly constraining the system's ability to expose unforeseen failure modes (\textit{unknown unknowns}) \cite{sridharan_2018, sakinala_2025}.

Historically, observability originates from modern control theory as formulated by Rudolf Kálmán \cite{kalman_1959}: a mathematical measure of how well internal system states can be inferred from knowledge of its external outputs \cite{songtab_1984}. In software systems, this concept has evolved into an engineering discipline centered on four complementary telemetry pillars \cite{kosinska_2023, bhatia_2025}. \textbf{Metrics} provide numeric, aggregated measurements of resource utilization, throughput, or error rates captured over uniform time intervals (e.g., CPU saturation, memory resident set size, request latency percentiles) \cite{ellingwood_2017}. \textbf{Logs} offer discrete, timestamped textual records that capture contextual execution details and state changes within individual services, providing indispensable forensic data for post-mortem debugging \cite{sre_2016}. \textbf{Distributed Traces} supply structured representations of end-to-end request journeys across microservice boundaries, tracking causally linked spans that record execution latencies and inter-service metadata \cite{otel_primer_2025}. Finally, \textbf{Events} capture asynchronous lifecycle transitions and operational alerts produced by the container runtime, Linux kernel, or Kubernetes control plane (e.g., pod evictions, OOM kills, deployment rollouts) \cite{hartikainen_2024}. As Hartikainen \cite{hartikainen_2024} notes, the mere collection of these four telemetry sources does not automatically render a platform observable; true observability is achieved only when these streams are semantically correlated and converted into actionable diagnostic intelligence.

Although frequently conflated in industrial vernacular, monitoring and observability represent distinct operational paradigms (Fig.~\ref{fig:obs-vs-mon}). Monitoring relies on predefined thresholds and predetermined metrics to verify whether a system is behaving as expected, detecting known failure modes (\textit{known knowns} and \textit{known unknowns}). Conversely, observability provides the semantic depth and cross-cutting trace context required to explore why and where unprecedented failures occur in complex distributed architectures (\textit{unknown unknowns}). Thus, monitoring is an operational subset and prerequisite of observability \cite{sridharan_2018}.

\subsection{Telemetry Instrumentation Levels in Kubernetes}
Telemetry generation in Kubernetes-orchestrated applications occurs across distinct abstraction layers, each presenting inherent trade-offs between semantic visibility and computational overhead. At the \textbf{Code/Application Level}, instrumentation is embedded directly within application logic using language-specific SDKs (e.g., OpenTelemetry SDK, Micrometer, Prometheus client libraries) to capture domain-specific metrics and fine-grained trace spans. At the \textbf{Platform/Mesh Level}, telemetry acquisition is decoupled from application code by injecting transparent sidecar proxies (e.g., Envoy) orchestrated by a service mesh (e.g., Istio), allowing ingress and egress HTTP/gRPC communication to be monitored for golden signals (latency, traffic, errors) without developer intervention. At the \textbf{Host/Node Level}, telemetry daemons deployed as Kubernetes DaemonSets—prominently the Prometheus Node Exporter and cAdvisor—poll Linux host virtual filesystems (\texttt{/proc} and \texttt{/sys}), kernel control groups (\texttt{cgroups}), and container runtime sockets to expose node- and container-level resource utilization. Finally, at the \textbf{Kernel Level (eBPF)}, sandboxed Extended Berkeley Packet Filter programs hook non-intrusively into system calls, socket buffers, and TCP/IP stack events, extracting high-throughput network and process telemetry with negligible runtime overhead and zero binary modifications \cite{rise_2023, faseeha_2025}.

\subsection{Related Work and Maturity Models in Observability}
Several secondary studies have examined aspects of microservice monitoring and observability. Kosińska et al. \cite{kosinska_2023} conducted a systematic literature review covering 2018 to 2022, classifying cloud-native observability approaches across DevOps life cycles and microservice requirements. Usman et al. \cite{usman_2022} surveyed observability practices in distributed edge and container environments, exploring telemetry data types and tool fragmentation. Faseeha et al. \cite{faseeha_2025} mapped observability across deployment paradigms, emphasizing service meshes and eBPF. Gomes et al. \cite{gomes_2025} conducted a systematic mapping of microservice observability with a specific focus on autoscaling controllers. While these secondary studies provide valuable macro-level overviews, they primarily catalog tools or synthesize broad architectural taxonomies without establishing formal maturity progressions or evaluating cross-layer interoperability bottlenecks in production Kubernetes clusters.

To structure evolutionary capability in complex software domains, maturity models have emerged as established design artifacts in Information Systems and Software Engineering \cite{wendler_2012}. Foundational research by de Bruin et al. \cite{debruin_2005} established the multi-phase lifecycle for maturity model development—spanning domain scoping, design, population, testing, and deployment. Becker, Knackstedt, and Pöppelbuß \cite{becker_2009} formalized this process into a rigorous procedural model governed by design requirements, mandating comparative analysis against prior artifacts, iterative construct refinement, and empirical validation. Furthermore, Pöppelbuß and Röglinger \cite{poppelbuss_2011} established design principles requiring maturity models to articulate distinct, mutually exclusive capability levels, well-defined progression criteria, and clear demarcation between descriptive and prescriptive uses. As Wendler \cite{wendler_2012} and Helgesson et al. \cite{helgesson_2012} observed in systematic mappings, maturity models are vital for benchmarking process capabilities, provided they are grounded in empirical evidence and systematic extraction rather than speculative heuristics.

In the domain of telemetry and distributed systems, researchers have increasingly applied these design principles to formalize observability adoption \cite{valbusa_2025, agarwal_2022, nethala_2025}. Valbusa \cite{valbusa_2025} grounds the Observability Maturity Model (OMM) in capability maturity principles, demonstrating that observability is not an isolated technology rollout but an evolutionary engineering discipline requiring progressive alignment between multi-pillar instrumentation, diagnostic workflows, and cultural adoption. Agarwal \cite{agarwal_2022} outlines a four-stage progression from basic infrastructure monitoring with static thresholds to cross-service telemetry correlation, SLO-driven reliability with error budgets, and predictive AI-augmented self-healing architectures. Similarly, Nethala \cite{nethala_2025} outlines enterprise observability implementation guidelines, framing maturity as an operational transition from siloed, single-metric monitoring toward integrated multi-tiered pipelines combining OpenTelemetry context propagation, tiered data storage, and automated anomaly detection to reduce Mean Time to Recovery (MTTR). In data-intensive domains, Patel \cite{patel_2025} formulates a specialized maturity model for big data pipeline observability across basic, advanced, and predictive stages, while Amgothu et al. \cite{amgothu_2025} conceptualize adaptive observability and AIOps integration in cloud-scale DevOps environments.

Despite these pioneering contributions, existing maturity models remain either broad enterprise guidelines \cite{nethala_2025, amgothu_2025} or high-level conceptual taxonomies \cite{agarwal_2022, valbusa_2025, patel_2025}. Notably, none of them systematically derives a maturity framework contextualized to the operational realities of Kubernetes—such as container ephemerality, kernel-space eBPF telemetry hooks, OTLP-Prometheus semantic translation nuances, and autonomous pod scaling. Grounded in the design science principles of Becker et al. \cite{becker_2009} and Pöppelbuß and Röglinger \cite{poppelbuss_2011}, this paper bridges this gap by systematically deriving the Observability Maturity Model (OMM) from the empirical evidence extracted across our peer-reviewed literature corpus.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

This study employs the Rapid Review (RR) methodology formulated by Cartaxo et al. \cite{cartaxo_2018} and guided by evidence-based software engineering principles \cite{kitchenham_2007, wohlin_2012}. RRs optimize search, screening, and data extraction procedures to deliver rigorous scientific evidence in a timely manner, bridging the gap between academic research and practical engineering decision-making in rapidly evolving technology landscapes. The review was structured into three consecutive phases: planning, performing, and extraction/reporting.

\subsection{Planning Phase and Review Protocol}
During planning, a formal review protocol was registered and managed using the \textit{Parsifal} online platform. The protocol established the research questions, explicit search strings, multi-database adaptation syntax, study selection criteria, and data extraction forms.

\textbf{Search Strategy and Query Formulation:} The primary search string was constructed using Boolean operators connecting the core conceptual dimensions of the research questions:
\begin{quote}
\small
\texttt{("cloud native" OR "cloud-native" OR "microservices") AND ("observability" OR "monitoring" OR "tracing" OR "logging" OR "metrics" OR "events") AND ("kubernetes" OR "k8s") AND ("open source" OR "open-source")}
\end{quote}

The search applied full-text indexing across four major scientific databases in Computer Science: IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, SpringerLink, and ScienceDirect. The use of full-text filtering was justified because in rapidly evolving infrastructure topics like cloud-native observability, technical architectures, open-source tool implementations, and Kubernetes deployment specifics are frequently detailed in the system architecture, implementation, or evaluation sections rather than solely in the abstract. Specific query adaptations were implemented to respect individual search syntax rules: for IEEE Xplore, queries were bounded within the \texttt{"Full Text \& Metadata"} scope; for the ACM Digital Library, mandatory term groupings were enforced; for SpringerLink, structured Boolean keyword matching was scoped to English peer-reviewed publications; and for ScienceDirect, clauses were adapted to search syntax limits while preserving the core conceptual terms.

The temporal window was defined from \textbf{January 2018 to October 2025}, capturing the evolution of Kubernetes into the industry-standard orchestrator and the emergence of modern CNCF observability standards (e.g., OpenTelemetry, OpenMetrics).

\textbf{Eligibility Criteria:} To ensure reproducible and unbiased study selection, six Inclusion Criteria (IC) were established: (IC01) primary focus on software observability or telemetry architectures; (IC02) context addressing cloud-native applications or containerized microservices; (IC03) explicit evaluation or deployment on Kubernetes; (IC04) analysis, proposal, or benchmarking of open-source tools, frameworks, or reference architectures; (IC05) publications dated between January 2018 and October 2025; and (IC06) full-text manuscripts written in English.

Conversely, five Exclusion Criteria (EC) were defined: (EC01) duplicate records indexed across multiple queried databases; (EC02) informal, non-peer-reviewed gray literature, corporate blogs, vendor whitepapers, and promotional product materials; (EC03) studies focused exclusively on conventional hardware or network monitoring without multi-pillar observability concepts; (EC04) articles where observability or Kubernetes is mentioned only tangentially as a secondary testing utility, workload generator, or peripheral evaluation benchmark; and (EC05) short papers, posters, and extended abstracts with fewer than 4 pages lacking methodological details or validation.

\textit{Clarification on Preprints and Academic Theses:} Following evidence-based guidelines for Rapid Reviews \cite{cartaxo_2018}, formal scientific preprints and institutional master's/doctoral theses identified through citation graph expansion were eligible when presenting complete empirical methodologies and architectural models meeting IC01–IC04 (e.g., Araujo et al. \cite{araujo_24} and Hartikainen \cite{hartikainen_2024}), strictly distinguishing them from commercial opinion material excluded under EC02.

\subsection{Research Questions}
The Rapid Review investigated four research questions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ01:} What are the essential components of an observability architecture for Kubernetes-based cloud-native applications?
    \item \textbf{RQ02:} What reference architectures or observability frameworks for Kubernetes-based cloud-native environments have been proposed in the literature?
    \item \textbf{RQ03:} What open-source tools are used or recommended to implement the different components of a Kubernetes-based observability architecture for cloud-native applications?
    \item \textbf{RQ04:} What research gaps and future directions have been identified in the literature on open-source observability architectures for Kubernetes-based cloud-native applications?
\end{itemize}

The study search and selection process was conducted systematically following the PRISMA guidelines (\textit{Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses}), as summarized in Table~\ref{tab:prisma_flow}.

The primary search yielded 312 raw records across the four databases. Following duplicate removal ($N = 58$), 254 unique records underwent title and abstract screening by the primary researcher, resulting in the exclusion of 208 irrelevant studies. The remaining 46 candidate studies were examined in full text. Of these, 38 were excluded with documented reasons: 14 under EC02/EC05 (short papers or commercial literature), 15 under EC03 (generic monitoring without observability pillars), and 9 under EC04 (observability mentioned only tangentially). This left 8 primary studies directly from the database query.

To address search string boundaries and discover influential unindexed works, citation graph exploration was performed using \textit{Connected Papers} alongside backward and forward snowballing seeded from seminal papers \cite{kosinska_2023, usman_2022}. This process identified 2 additional eligible studies (\cite{araujo_24, hartikainen_2024}), yielding a final synthesis corpus of 10 primary studies ($N = 10$).

\textbf{Cross-Validation and Conflict Resolution:} While initial title and abstract filtering was conducted by the primary researcher, full-text eligibility reviews and data extractions were verified through paired cross-validation by a secondary author. Disagreements regarding study categorization or inclusion were resolved through joint consensus meetings with the senior supervising researcher to ensure consistency.

\subsection{Data Extraction and Synthesis}
For each of the 10 included primary studies, data were extracted into structured Parsifal forms and compiled into an audit extraction matrix: study metadata, addressed research questions (RQ1–RQ4), telemetry pillars analyzed, investigated open-source tools, proposed architectural patterns, identified research gaps, and recommended future directions. The extracted evidence was qualitatively synthesized to construct the comparative tables, the maturity model, and the research roadmap.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:validity}
To ensure methodological transparency, potential threats to validity were categorized and mitigated according to Wohlin et al. \cite{wohlin_2012} and Cartaxo et al. \cite{cartaxo_2018}.

\textbf{Construct Validity} concerns whether the operational measures reflect the theoretical constructs under review. Search strings might inadvertently miss relevant studies using divergent terminology. We mitigated this by combining synonyms across cloud-native computing, container orchestration, and telemetry pillars, and by employing citation graph exploration (\textit{Connected Papers}) to identify adjacent studies.

\textbf{Internal Validity} addresses potential researcher bias during study screening and data extraction. The time constraints of Rapid Reviews require single-reviewer screening at the title/abstract stage \cite{cartaxo_2018}. We mitigated this risk by enforcing rigid IC/EC criteria, conducting dual cross-validation during the full-text evaluation phase, and maintaining fully audit-trailed screening logs in the replication package.

\textbf{Conclusion Validity} relates to the reproducibility and reliability of the synthesized conclusions. Because primary studies reported diverse qualitative frameworks and heterogeneous benchmarks (varying across CPU overhead, memory footprint, and network throughput), a quantitative meta-analysis was not feasible. We mitigated this threat by structuring conclusions qualitatively through thematic extraction, standardizing terminology, and validating findings against established software engineering maturity frameworks.

\textbf{External Validity} concerns the generalizability of the findings to broader cloud-native ecosystems. The review focused on four indexed academic databases (IEEE, ACM, Springer, ScienceDirect) between 2018 and October 2025. While this captured representative peer-reviewed software engineering literature, findings may not encompass all non-academic, closed-source enterprise stacks or emerging tools developed outside the academic literature.

\section{Findings and Discussions}
\label{sec:findings}

This section presents the synthesis of findings structured according to the four research questions, examining essential components, reference architectures, open-source toolchains, and future research directions.

\subsection{Essential Architectural Components (RQ01)}
The literature establishes that an effective cloud-native observability architecture requires coordinated capabilities across multiple functional layers \cite{kosinska_2023, usman_2022, faseeha_2025}. The core data foundation comprises metrics, logs, distributed traces, and platform events. However, primary studies converge on the principle that collecting these pillars in silos yields limited diagnostic value; high-value observability requires cross-pillar correlation at the point of ingestion and analysis \cite{gomes_2025, araujo_24}.

Functionally, the architecture must support the end-to-end Observability Data Life Cycle (ODLC) \cite{araujo_24, han_2024}: (1) \textit{Collection and Instrumentation}, acquiring raw telemetry via language SDKs, sidecars, daemon exporters, and kernel probes; (2) \textit{Processing and Pipeline Buffering}, performing filtering, label enrichment, batching, and dynamic sampling; (3) \textit{Specialized Storage}, distributing telemetry into specialized time-series, column-oriented, or inverted-index databases; and (4) \textit{Correlation and Visualization}, synthesizing multidimensional queries, service maps, and root-cause localization graphs.

\subsection{Reference Architectures and Frameworks (RQ02)}
A major finding is the noticeable absence of formal, standardized reference architectures in the scientific literature specifically designed for Kubernetes \cite{araujo_24, kosinska_2023}. Although academic works propose conceptual lifecycle models \cite{araujo_24, han_2024}, production environments rely on a community-driven \textit{de facto} architectural pattern.

This \textit{de facto} pattern is structured across four continuous architectural tiers. In the \textit{Agent Tier}, collectors are deployed as cluster-wide Kubernetes DaemonSets (e.g., Fluentd, Node Exporter) or integrated via pod sidecars (e.g., Envoy proxies) and kernel probes (e.g., Cilium eBPF) to intercept runtime signals. In the \textit{Aggregation and Routing Tier}, centralized pipelines (such as the OpenTelemetry Collector or Vector) receive heterogeneous telemetry protocols, normalize schemas, apply batching, and route payloads to designated backends. In the \textit{Specialized Datastore Tier}, data streams are directed into decoupled storage engines tailored to distinct telemetry shapes, such as time-series databases (Prometheus, Mimir) for numeric metrics, chunked-index storage (Loki) for unstructured logs, and distributed trace stores (Jaeger, Tempo). Finally, in the \textit{Query and Presentation Tier}, unified dashboarding interfaces (primarily Grafana) provide single-pane-of-glass visualization, threshold-based alerting, and interactive query exploration across all underlying data stores.

\subsection{Open-Source Toolchains and Interoperability (RQ03)}
The review identifies a mature, though fragmented, open-source toolchain across the Kubernetes ecosystem. OpenTelemetry (OTel) has rapidly established itself as the leading vendor-neutral framework for telemetry collection and distributed tracing representation via the OpenTelemetry Protocol (OTLP) \cite{kosinska_2023, faseeha_2025}. However, OTel is not yet a universal standard across all telemetry dimensions in production. 

For metric scraping and alerting, Prometheus and its OpenMetrics format remain the dominant and entrenched standard in Kubernetes deployments, with client libraries widely embedded across enterprise microservices \cite{usman_2022, faseeha_2025}. Bidirectional interoperability between OpenTelemetry and Prometheus still presents nontrivial challenges, including lossy metric conversions (e.g., differences in histogram bucket representations, quantile aggregations, and label naming semantics) \cite{hartikainen_2024}. 

In the logging domain, Grafana Loki and Fluentd lead Kubernetes log management, while Jaeger and Grafana Tempo dominate distributed tracing. eBPF-based frameworks such as Cilium and Hubble provide non-intrusive kernel-level network tracing and security observability without application code modification \cite{faseeha_2025}. Despite this maturity, primary studies emphasize that these tools are rarely plug-and-play; engineering teams face significant cognitive load and operational overhead in configuring collectors, managing cardinality, and maintaining bespoke glue pipelines \cite{kosinska_2023, usman_2022}.

The synthesis exposes three pervasive challenges: (1) stack fragmentation and the absence of unified semantic schemas, which prevent automated end-to-end tracing across disaggregated components; (2) excessive computational overhead (CPU, memory footprint, and network saturation) imposed by full-fidelity telemetry collection in high-throughput or resource-constrained environments \cite{zanatta_23, araujo_24}; and (3) telemetry overload, where the sheer volume of unstructured metrics and logs overwhelms human operators during complex incident response \cite{han_2024, deng_2024}.

\subsection{Observability Maturity Model (OMM)}
To provide software architects with a grounded diagnostic instrument for cloud-native platforms, we formalize the Observability Maturity Model (OMM), illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:obs-arch}. Grounded in the design science principles of Becker et al. \cite{becker_2009}, de Bruin et al. \cite{debruin_2005}, and Pöppelbuß and Röglinger \cite{poppelbuss_2011}, and building upon the observability capability foundations \cite{valbusa_2025, agarwal_2022, nethala_2025, patel_2025} reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:background}, our model systematically derives its evolutionary stages from the empirical evidence synthesized across our 10 primary studies ($N = 10$). Following the procedural guidelines for maturity model development \cite{becker_2009, wendler_2012}, the OMM establishes mutually exclusive capability levels and actionable progression criteria tailored to the dynamic realities of Kubernetes clusters, overcoming the limitations of previous models that remained either abstract or disconnected from containerized orchestration. At \textbf{Level 1 (Non-Existent)}, applications operate with minimal or no telemetry instrumentation, leaving failures to be detected reactively through user complaints or catastrophic container termination. In \textbf{Level 2 (Initial / Ad-Hoc)}, unstandardized logs and localized metrics are captured on individual container instances or developer nodes, remaining siloed without centralized retention or cross-component correlation. In \textbf{Level 3 (Managed)}, multi-pillar telemetry (metrics, logs, traces) is systematically ingested into centralized datastores via standardized agents (e.g., Prometheus scrapers, Fluentd DaemonSets) and paired with threshold-based alerting. Progressing to \textbf{Level 4 (Optimized)}, telemetry data is semantically linked across microservice boundaries; unified visualization platforms (e.g., Grafana) enable contextual cross-navigation between metrics, trace spans, and associated log entries for rapid root-cause isolation. Finally, at \textbf{Level 5 (Predictive / Autonomic)}, the complete telemetry tuple is continuously processed by machine learning models and AIOps engines (e.g., Graph Attention Networks \cite{han_2024}) to anticipate performance degradation, automate anomaly localization, and trigger proactive Kubernetes autoscaling or self-healing workflows before service-level objectives (SLOs) are breached.

\subsection{Strategic Research Roadmap}
To address the limitations documented in Table~\ref{tab:related_works}, Figure~\ref{fig:research-road} outlines a strategic research roadmap structured around three advancement pillars. The first pillar, \textbf{Semantic Interoperability and Low-Overhead Instrumentation}, addresses the transition beyond fragmented collectors toward unified, non-intrusive telemetry pipelines. This requires deep integration of eBPF with OpenTelemetry standards to achieve kernel-to-application visibility with negligible CPU overhead, alongside formal standardization of metric histogram translations and label mappings between OTLP and Prometheus. The second pillar, \textbf{AIOps for Multimodal Telemetry Correlation}, focuses on developing scalable graph neural networks and correlation algorithms capable of processing the complete telemetry tuple $O = \{M, L, T, E\}$ to localize root causes in ephemeral container clusters without manual query construction \cite{han_2024}. The third pillar, \textbf{Autonomic and Sustainable Observability (GreenOps \& SecOps)}, targets Level 5 maturity by integrating real-time telemetry into autonomic Kubernetes control loops for energy-aware workload scheduling, carbon footprint reduction, and runtime threat detection \cite{deng_2024, kosinska_2023}.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This Rapid Review synthesized the state of the art in open-source observability architectures and tools for Kubernetes-based cloud-native applications. Our findings confirm that while a robust open-source toolchain exists—anchored by OpenTelemetry, Prometheus, Grafana, Loki, Jaeger, and eBPF—the domain lacks formal, standardized reference architectures. In practice, organizations rely on a loosely coupled \textit{de facto} pattern that incurs significant operational friction, data silos, and interoperability challenges.

To bridge these gaps, this work contributed: (1) a systematic analysis of Kubernetes telemetry layers and open-source tools; (2) the formalization of the Observability Maturity Model (OMM) across five evolutionary levels to guide architectural assessments; and (3) a strategic research roadmap prioritizing semantic interoperability, multimodal AIOps correlation, and sustainable autonomic observability. By addressing these challenges, researchers and software architects can advance beyond fragmented, reactive tooling toward cohesive, intelligent, and low-overhead observability platforms.
